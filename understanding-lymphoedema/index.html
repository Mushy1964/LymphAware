Understanding Lymphoedema

What is lymphoedema?

Lymphoedema is a long-term, progressive condition that causes swelling in the body's tissues when the lymphatic system is damaged or unable to drain fluid effectively. It can affect almost any part of the body and may cause discomfort, heaviness, reduced mobility, skin changes and a greater risk of infection. For many people, lymphoedema also has a wider impact on daily life, confidence, clothing choices, work, relationships and emotional wellbeing, often requiring ongoing management such as compression, skin care, exercise and monitoring to help control symptoms and reduce complications.

More than simply swelling

Lymphoedema can affect different parts of the body and can vary considerably from one person to another.

For some people it may affect mobility, comfort, clothing, the need to wear compression garments, skin care or everyday activities.

The way lymphoedema affects one person may therefore be very different from the way it affects somebody else.

Why understanding matters

Lymphoedema is not always visible or well understood.

A person may need additional time, consideration or assistance even when their condition is not immediately obvious.

Lymphoedema is individual

LymphAware ID does not assume that everyone living with lymphoedema has the same needs or experiences.

Different locations

Lymphoedema can affect limbs and other areas of the body, depending on the individual and underlying cause.

Different needs

Some people may use compression garments, require assistance with mobility or have other practical considerations.

Different experiences

Symptoms, treatment needs and day-to-day impact can vary significantly, which is why patient-selected information is important.

How lymphoedema can progress

Stages of lymphoedema

Lymphoedema is commonly described using four clinical stages, from Stage 0 to Stage 3. The stages describe changes in swelling and the tissues over time, but the condition can affect people very differently and does not always progress in the same way.

Important: these stages are a general guide rather than a prediction of what will happen to an individual. Lymphoedema may affect different parts of the body, and symptoms and severity can vary considerably from person to person.

Living with lymphoedema

Four cornerstones of self-care

Looking after lymphoedema often means combining several approaches. A lymphoedema specialist can help you choose what is appropriate for your body and your needs.

Skin care

Keep skin clean and moisturised, and check for cuts or changes. Healthy skin helps reduce the risk of infection, including cellulitis.

Compression

Professionally fitted garments or wraps can help limit fluid build-up. Your specialist can advise on the right type and how to wear it.

Movement and exercise

Gentle, regular movement uses your muscles to support lymph flow and mobility. Activities can be adapted to your ability and affected area.

Lymphatic drainage

Manual lymphatic drainage (MLD) is a gentle technique provided by a trained therapist. They may teach you simpler self-massage, called simple lymphatic drainage (SLD).

How the lymphatic system helps

Cellulitis awareness

Cellulitis is an acute spreading infection of the skin and tissues beneath it and is an important complication of lymphoedema. It can cause pain, warmth, increased swelling and redness or inflammation, and some people may also feel generally unwell or develop a fever.

Cellulitis can be difficult to diagnose and its treatment in someone with lymphoedema may need particular clinical consideration. If you think you may have cellulitis, seek medical advice promptly.

This is awareness information only. LymphAware ID does not diagnose cellulitis, provide treatment instructions or replace professional medical advice or emergency care.

LymphAware ID supports communication

LymphAware ID is not intended to diagnose lymphoedema or tell healthcare professionals how an individual should be treated.

Its role is to help a person communicate relevant information they have chosen to make available.

Clinical assessment and professional medical advice remain important whenever healthcare decisions are required.
